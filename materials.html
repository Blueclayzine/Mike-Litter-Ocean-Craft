MATERIALS

The good stuff.

We are so accustomed to things constructed from man-made super materials that we rarely question if these toxic materials are truly necessary, and we also assume products made from natural materials are inferior. But for things with few moving parts and a simple mandate—like paddles, handplanes and surfboards—why not reconsider wood as the ideal material?

I have a garage full of carbon fiber canoes and foam surfboards. So I'm not a purist. But it stinks to feel passionate about our beloved oceans while at the same time using gear with dubious environmental credentials.

Also, I am smitten with trees. So it makes me happy to know about these enormous, living organisms that provide us such long-lasting material to work with. When we lock up that tree's inhaled carbon in a paddle, handplane or surfboard, we further extend wood's carbon storage lifespan.

I use a number of evergreen timbers from the West Coast: Sitka Spruce for its stiffness and superior strength to weight ratio, Douglas Fir for its toughness and flex, Western Red Cedar for its dark tones and light weight, and redwood (always reclaimed) for its beauty and ancient soul. Whenever possible I go for reclaimed wood, trees from second generation forests or plantation grown timbers. Old growth trees of these four species can be over a thousand years old and totally irreplaceable.

I also use North American hardwoods sparingly due to their heavier weight. Cherry is my favorite for handles and edgebanding. It is one of the most sustainable U.S. hardwoods due to its relatively fast rate of growth and strict forest management.

The MVP of the highly functional wooden ocean craft, however, is Paulownia, which I source from the Southern U.S. and from China. Paulownia is slightly heavier than Balsa but much stronger and more suitable for wet environments. I use it for the paddle blades and non load-bearing laminates in the paddle shafts. Paulownia is also fast growing and sustainably harvested in plantations where it absorbs carbon at high rates, giving it the distinction of being a carbon sink powerhouse.

TOOLS

Great tools make… great tools.

The more peaceful the build process, the more well-behaved the paddle, plane or board. Ocean craft are much like people in this way. I use a selection of traditional hand tools—spoke shaves, palm planes, travishers, coopers planes—borrowed from cask, chair and violin makers. The quiet work of hand planing and carving is so much sweeter than the ear splitting thrum of a power tool. When the work is scaled down, slow and quiet, the builder can tune into the wood's unique characteristics and limitations. This is what allows for an exquisite result.
